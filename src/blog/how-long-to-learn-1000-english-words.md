The calculation looks simple: learn ten words a day and finish 1,000 words in 100 days. The problem is that “finish” can mean seeing each word once, recognizing it in a quiz, remembering it a month later, or using it naturally in conversation. Those are very different achievements.

> **Direct answer:** At a sustainable pace of five to ten new words a day, a learner can study 1,000 words for the first time in about three to seven months. Building durable, usable knowledge usually takes longer because old words need repeated review, listening, sentence practice, and real use after the first exposure.

### A simple timeline based on daily pace

The table below estimates only the time needed to introduce 1,000 items. It does not include missed days or long-term consolidation.

| New words per day | Days for first exposure | Approximate time |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 3 | 334 | About 11 months |
| 5 | 200 | About 6.5 months |
| 8 | 125 | About 4 months |
| 10 | 100 | About 3.3 months |
| 15 | 67 | About 2.2 months |
| 20 | 50 | About 7 weeks |

The faster schedules are mathematically possible, but review grows as the list grows. Twenty new words today may create dozens or hundreds of review prompts later. If you reduce review quality to protect the daily number, the count becomes misleading.

### What does it mean to “learn” a word?

A learner may claim to know *charge* after memorising “চার্জ করা,” but the word has several common uses: charge a phone, charge a price, be in charge, face a charge, or rush forward. A beginner does not need every meaning immediately, but one translation does not equal complete knowledge.

For an everyday target word, aim to know:

- the written and spoken form;
- one useful meaning;
- the part of speech;
- one common phrase or collocation;
- one example sentence;
- the ability to recall it after a delay;
- the ability to understand it in new context.

Productive words need another layer: you should be able to retrieve and use them without seeing the English answer.

This makes vocabulary learning cumulative rather than linear. On day 60, you are not only learning new words. You are maintaining words from days 1 to 59.

### Why some words take much longer than others

Words differ in difficulty. A Bangla-speaking learner may remember a familiar loanword quickly but struggle with a short function word whose meaning changes by context. Difficulty can come from:

- multiple common meanings;
- irregular forms;
- unfamiliar pronunciation;
- confusing similarity to another word;
- abstract meaning;
- unusual spelling;
- restricted grammar patterns;
- collocations that do not translate directly into Bangla.

For example, *information* is conceptually familiar but causes a grammar problem because standard English usually treats it as uncountable. Learning “information = তথ্য” without learning **some information** and **a piece of information** leaves an important gap.

A realistic schedule therefore measures study load, not only item count. Five difficult words may require more work than fifteen transparent ones.

### Recognition can improve quickly; active use takes longer

If you review English on one side and Bangla on the other, you may recognize many words within weeks. Speaking is slower because it reverses the task. You must begin with an idea and retrieve the English form.

Try these two tests:

1. **Recognition test:** What does *borrow* mean?
2. **Production test:** How do you say “কারও কাছ থেকে কিছু সাময়িকভাবে নেওয়া” in English?

The second task is harder. It also resembles speaking more closely. A balanced learning plan should include both directions, plus sentences such as **Can I borrow your pen?** and the contrast with **lend**.

### A practical 20-minute daily routine

A manageable session can look like this:

#### Five minutes: review older words

Recall the answer before revealing it. Give more time to words you miss and less to words you retrieve easily.

#### Five minutes: learn five new words

For each word, identify one meaning, pronunciation, part of speech, and one natural phrase. Use the [1,000 common English words with Bangla meanings](https://rhosen.github.io/getmane/resources/1000-common-english-words-bangla/) as a structured source.

#### Five minutes: create or transform sentences

Write one personal sentence, then change its tense, subject, or context. For example:

- I **borrowed** a book yesterday.
- She often **borrows** my notes.
- Can I **borrow** this calculator?

#### Five minutes: speak without looking

Summarize your day and deliberately use two or three target words. The goal is retrieval, not a perfect speech.

At five words per day, you introduce 1,000 items in 200 study days. More importantly, the routine gives each word multiple kinds of contact.

### A faster 10-word plan without sacrificing review

Ten words per day can work when you already have a foundation and enough review time. Divide the words:

- five words for active use;
- five words mainly for recognition.

Deeply practise the active set. For the receptive set, learn enough to understand them in context and promote only the most useful words later. This reflects real vocabulary: you do not need to speak every word you can understand.

You can also learn through sentence resources instead of isolated cards. The [500 daily-use English sentences with Bangla meaning](https://rhosen.github.io/getmane/resources/500-daily-use-english-sentences-bangla/) repeatedly exposes common vocabulary inside useful patterns. The broader [1,200+ spoken English sentence resource](https://rhosen.github.io/getmane/resources/1200-spoken-english-sentences-bangla/) helps you connect vocabulary with situations.

### How to know whether your pace is too fast

Reduce new words when:

- your review queue keeps growing;
- you guess instead of recalling;
- yesterday’s words already feel unfamiliar;
- you have no time for reading, listening, or speaking;
- vocabulary study feels like maintaining a spreadsheet;
- you repeatedly confuse related items learned together.

A good pace leaves space for language use. Vocabulary is supposed to improve comprehension and expression, not consume every minute that could have been spent using English.

### Frequently asked questions

#### Can I learn 1,000 English words in one month?

You can expose yourself to roughly 33 words a day, but retaining and using all of them is a much harder goal. A one-month sprint may work for revision of partly familiar words, not for deep first-time learning of 1,000 unknown items.

#### Is five words a day too slow?

No. Five words a day produces more than 1,800 first exposures in a year before accounting for breaks. If you remember and use them, this is substantial progress.

#### How many times must I review a word?

There is no universal number. A concrete, familiar word may stick quickly; an abstract or confusing word may need many encounters. Review based on successful recall and real exposure rather than a fixed repetition count.

#### Should I study every day?

Regular study helps, but consistency does not require perfection. A lighter review day or occasional break is better than abandoning the system after an unrealistic schedule.

### Final takeaway

For most learners, three to seven months is a reasonable period for first study of 1,000 common words at five to ten per day. Durable knowledge continues developing after that. Count successful recall and useful sentences, not only completed list items.
