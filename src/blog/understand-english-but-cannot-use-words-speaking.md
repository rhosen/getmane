You hear a word in a video and understand it immediately. You see it in a book and know the Bangla meaning. Then, during conversation, the word disappears. A simpler word comes out, or you pause until the moment has passed. This does not mean the word was never learned. It means it is easier to recognize than to retrieve.

> **Direct answer:** You understand more English words than you can use because listening and reading provide the word for recognition, while speaking requires you to retrieve it from memory, choose the correct form, pronounce it, and fit it into a sentence quickly. To close the gap, practise production rather than adding only more input.

### Recognition and recall are different tasks

In reading, the word is visible. In listening, the speaker provides its sound. Your brain matches the input with stored knowledge. This is recognition.

Speaking begins with an idea. You must search memory for the English form. This is recall. Then you must manage grammar, pronunciation, word order, and the listener’s response at the same time.

Compare these tasks:

- **Recognition:** What does *recommend* mean?
- **Recall:** How do you say “পরামর্শ দেওয়া” in English?
- **Production:** Recommend a book to a friend and explain why.

Each step demands more control. A learner may succeed at the first and struggle with the third.

### Your receptive vocabulary is naturally larger

The words you understand in reading and listening form your receptive vocabulary. The words you can produce in speaking and writing form your productive vocabulary. The receptive set is usually larger because context helps recognition.

This gap also exists in a person’s first language. You may understand formal Bangla words in a newspaper without using them in daily conversation. In English, the gap feels more frustrating because you actively want to speak.

Do not respond by trying to activate every word. Select words that are frequent, personally useful, and suitable for your speaking level.

### You may know a meaning but not a usable pattern

A translation does not tell you how a word behaves. Suppose you know:

> **suggest = প্রস্তাব করা / পরামর্শ দেওয়া**

During speech, you may try **suggest me to go**, a pattern transferred from another structure. Standard English commonly uses:

- **I suggest going early.**
- **I suggest that we go early.**
- **She suggested a different plan.**

The word was not missing; the grammatical frame was. Learn vocabulary with the structures that carry it.

Other useful patterns include:

- **depend on**;
- **interested in**;
- **responsible for**;
- **avoid doing**;
- **decide to do**.

### Speaking creates time pressure

During private study, you may take twenty seconds to remember a word. Conversation rarely waits that long. You are also monitoring whether your grammar is correct, whether the listener understands, and what they may say next.

This pressure causes learners to return to highly available words such as *good, bad, thing, do,* and *make*. These words are not wrong, but overuse can hide a wider passive vocabulary.

Speed develops through repeated retrieval of small sets. Practise three to five target words in several short speaking tasks instead of trying to use twenty new words in one conversation.

### Translation can slow retrieval

A beginner often follows this path:

> idea → Bangla sentence → English translation → spoken sentence

The process is useful at first but becomes slow under pressure. Build direct links between situations and English phrases.

Instead of translating “আমার মনে হয়…” every time, retrieve complete frames:

- **I think…**
- **In my opinion…**
- **It seems to me that…**
- **As far as I know…**

Use the Bangla meaning to understand the pattern, then practise producing the English without looking.

### Perfectionism blocks words you already know

Some learners reject a usable sentence while searching for the perfect one. They want the exact advanced word, correct tense, ideal pronunciation, and elegant structure before speaking. The result is silence.

Use a repair strategy:

- say the simpler version first;
- describe the missing word;
- correct yourself if necessary;
- ask, **What is the word for…?**

For example, if *appointment* does not come, say **I have a time fixed to meet the doctor**. Communication continues, and the missing word becomes memorable because it solved a real need.

### How to activate a passive word

Use this six-step cycle.

#### 1. Choose one useful sense

Do not activate every dictionary definition at once. Select the meaning that appears in your life.

#### 2. Learn a phrase

For *available*, learn **available tomorrow**, **available online**, and **Is this available?**

#### 3. Retrieve from a prompt

Hide the word. Use a Bangla meaning, definition, picture, or question as the cue.

#### 4. Answer a personal question

“Are you available this evening?” creates meaningful production.

#### 5. Repeat in changed contexts

Use the word with different subjects, tenses, or situations.

#### 6. Reuse after a delay

Return to it in later speaking, not only immediately after studying.

### Three speaking exercises that build retrieval

#### Timed category recall

Choose a topic such as school, travel, or work. Name related words for sixty seconds, then use five of them in connected sentences. This moves vocabulary from list form into speech.

#### Retell with target words

Read a short paragraph, close it, and retell the idea using three selected words. Do not memorise the original wording.

#### Repeated answer

Answer the same question three times. On the second and third answer, add one target phrase or improve precision. Repetition reduces cognitive load and frees attention for vocabulary.

### Stop collecting words temporarily

If your receptive vocabulary is much larger than your active vocabulary, a short activation period may help more than another list. For one week:

- add very few new words;
- select twenty known but unavailable words;
- practise them in phrases;
- answer questions aloud;
- record and listen to yourself;
- notice which words still fail under pressure.

### Frequently asked questions

#### Is it normal to understand English but speak poorly?

Yes. Comprehension often develops faster because input supplies the language. Speaking needs separate practice in retrieval, pronunciation, sentence construction, and interaction.

#### Should I stop reading and listening?

No. Input supplies models and reinforces vocabulary. Change the balance: continue understandable input while adding deliberate output and recall.

#### How long does it take to activate a word?

There is no fixed time. Frequent, concrete, personally relevant words may become available quickly. Abstract words or words with complex patterns may need many successful uses across weeks.

#### Is talking to myself useful?

Yes, especially for low-pressure retrieval. Describe actions, explain a plan, or answer common questions. Also seek interactive practice because real conversation requires listening and response.

### Final takeaway

The problem is not that your vocabulary has disappeared. It is stored in a form that supports recognition more easily than production. Practise a smaller set through phrases, recall, personal answers, and repeated speaking until the route from idea to English becomes faster.
